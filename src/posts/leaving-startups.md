For about a year I was a founder. I started **Ascade** in June 2025 with a close friend, Pius, and my time there ended in May 2026. We were accepted into Y Combinator's F25 batch, and I chose not to go, because I wanted to do research. This isn't a story about something going badly, and it isn't a story about regret. I really like startups. I plan to join one again.

## What we built

The earliest prototypes in my files are from September and October 2025. The first was a pipeline that turned plain-English specifications into **formal postconditions**: you write "sort a list of integers" and it produces the mathematical statement a correct sort must satisfy, with help from a language model and an SMT solver (Z3) to check what it generated. The second was a desktop app for designing a program's **behavior** as a graph over its abstract syntax tree and exporting contracts from it.

Those ideas grew into the part I'm proudest of: **formally verified software**. We shipped a verified payment processor that reached $25K in monthly revenue, won a DARPA ERIS contract and other federal work to build verified access control for AI agents, and trained a small 8B model that set the state of the art on formal-verification benchmarks, beating much larger frontier models. (I wrote [a bit about the idea behind it](verification-loop.html), with something you can break.)

## What I loved

Startups are fun, in a way that's hard to explain to someone who hasn't done one. You move fast. You have to make assumptions with half the information, ship, and find out in a week whether you were right. The loop between an idea and a customer's reaction is short. I picked up a lot from that.

## So why leave?

Mostly it came down to what I wanted to find out about myself.

I want to see how far I can push my own thinking: reach my intellectual limit, and see how much I can contribute to the field from there. Research is where I get to ask open-ended questions and follow them wherever they go. In the startup, the problems were clear and so were the ways to make money, and the work was exploring branches that were already visible and finding the one that paid best. That's real, valuable work. But in research I get to ask what the tree even looks like, and sometimes grow a branch nobody has tried. Right now that pull is stronger than anything else.

The areas I care about (diffusion models, AI for science, and increasingly the theory underneath) are ones where *finding the right question* is most of the work. It's also an easier place to be honest: a result either holds up or it doesn't.

## What happens next

I expect to come back. I don't think of this as leaving startups so much as taking the long way around: pushing on research until I find where my own limits are, and then bringing whatever I've learned to a team that needs it. The best version of me in a startup is probably one who has spent a few years working out how to ask better questions.

And I learned to value people over plans. Pius is still one of my closest friends. If you can change direction and stay close, that's the best outcome you could ask for.
