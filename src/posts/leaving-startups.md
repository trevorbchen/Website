I got into Y Combinator's F25 batch with Ascade, the company I started with a close friend and then-cofounder, Pius, and I've been in startups in some form ever since. I'm still proud of what we built: formally verified software for AI agents, real customers, federal contracts. This isn't a story about it going badly.

The surprising part is that I stepped back anyway, and that Pius is still one of my closest friends.

## What I noticed

Mostly it came down to **how I like to spend my days.**

In a company, a lot of the time goes to things that aren't the problem: fundraising, positioning, who's in the room. Not everyone you meet along the way is chasing the same unvarnished picture of how the world works. In my interactions with investors and other founders, I often felt the conversation was more about how a thing would be *seen* than about whether it was *true*. That's not malice, it's the game. But I didn't enjoy playing it.

What I do love is **open-ended questions** and following them to see where they go. I'm better at that than at almost anything else.

There was another difference. In startups the problems were clear and the ways to make money were clear. The work was *exploring the known branches* of a tree and finding which one pays best. That's a real skill and valuable work. But in research I get to ask what the tree even looks like, and sometimes grow a branch nobody has tried. That's the part I'd do for free.

## Why research

Research suits how I think. It rewards the habit I've been building since I was young: figure out what you don't know, get a handle on it, and ask a better question than the last one. The domains I care about, diffusion models and AI for science in particular (and increasingly the theory underneath), are open questions where *finding the right question* is most of the work.

It's also an easier life to be honest in. A result either holds up or it doesn't.

## What I took with me

Startups taught me things I'd never have learned in a lab: how to ship, how to make something other people rely on, how to be responsible for a working thing. I carry that into research in small ways: I care whether code runs, whether numbers are reproducible, whether a result matters to someone outside the paper.

It also taught me to value people. Pius and I are as close as ever. If you can disagree about the direction of your life and stay friends, that's the better outcome.

I'd tell anyone who feels the same pull: it's fine to love the question more than the company.
