For about a year I was a founder. I started **Ascade** in June 2025 with a close friend, Pius, and my time there ended in May 2026. I was accepted into Y Combinator's F25 batch and chose not to go, because I wanted to do research. This isn't a story about something going badly, and it isn't a story about regret. It's about finding out which kind of problem I actually like.

## What we built

The earliest prototypes in my files are from September and October 2025. The first was a pipeline that turned plain-English specifications into **formal postconditions**: you write "sort a list of integers" and it produces the mathematical statement a correct sort must satisfy, with help from a language model and an SMT solver (Z3) to check what it generated. The second was a desktop app for designing a program's **behavior** as a graph over its abstract syntax tree and exporting contracts from it.

Those ideas grew into the part I'm proudest of: **formally verified software**. We shipped a verified payment processor that reached $25K in monthly revenue, won a DARPA ERIS contract and other federal work to build verified access control for AI agents, and trained a small 8B model that set the state of the art on formal-verification benchmarks, beating much larger frontier models.

I learned a huge amount about shipping something other people rely on.

## So why leave?

Mostly it came down to **how I like to spend my days.**

In a company, a lot of the time goes to things that aren't the problem: fundraising, positioning, who's in the room. Not everyone you meet along the way shares the same unvarnished picture of how the world works. In my conversations with investors and other founders, I often felt the discussion was more about how something would be *seen* than about whether it was *true*. That isn't malice, it's the game. I just didn't enjoy playing it.

What I do love is **open-ended questions**, and following them to see where they go. That's the thing I'm best at.

There was another difference. In the startup, the problems were clear and so were the ways to make money. The work was *exploring branches that were already visible* and finding the one that paid best. That is real, valuable work. But in research I get to ask what the tree even looks like, and sometimes grow a branch nobody has tried. That's the part I'd do for free.

## Why research

Research fits how I think. It rewards the habit I've been building since I was young: work out what you don't know, get a handle on it, and ask a better question than the last one. The areas I care about (diffusion models, AI for science, and increasingly the theory underneath) are ones where *finding the right question* is most of the work. It's also an easier life to be honest in: a result either holds up or it doesn't.

## What I took with me

Startups taught me things I'd never have learned in a lab. I care whether code actually runs, whether numbers reproduce, and whether a result matters to someone outside the paper.

And I learned to value people over plans. Pius is still one of my closest friends. If you can change direction and stay close, that's the best outcome you could ask for.

If you feel the same pull, here's what I'd tell you: it's fine to love the question more than the company.
